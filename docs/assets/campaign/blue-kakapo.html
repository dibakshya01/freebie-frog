You open the queue at the start of a shift, and the number at the top has four digits in it. Three thousand alerts on a quiet day, nearer four and a half on a loud one. You know, with the dull certainty of experience, that you will not reach the bottom of it. Nobody does. Somewhere between forty and sixty of every hundred will go uninvestigated, and close to half of what you do read turns out to be a false alarm that ate an hour you did not have. And the thought that sits behind all of it, the one you try not to look at directly, is that the single alert that actually matters is in there somewhere, quiet and well behaved, wearing the same grey as the rest.

The queue does not care that you are tired. That is the whole trouble with it.

The obvious move, the one every vendor will sell you, is to point a clever machine at the pile and let it thin the herd. Fair enough. Tier-1 triage is the agreed first thing to automate, and a tireless first reader is exactly what an exhausted team needs. But look closely at what is actually on offer, and a second problem rises up behind the first. The capable AI-SOC products today are closed, cloud-only services. They ship your security telemetry off to someone else's data centre, bill you on consumption pricing that moves when they please, and reason inside a box you are not permitted to open. You are handed a verdict and invited to believe it.

And here is the thing about a security verdict you cannot check. It offers no reassurance at all. It is a second alert, wearing a nicer suit.

So I built blue-kakapo. It is an open-source, self-hostable platform in which a swarm of fourteen specialised agents, coordinated by a deterministic orchestrator, does the Tier-1 first pass. It runs entirely inside your own network, on any model you care to point it at, and it shows its work for every verdict it reaches.

That last clause is the whole design, so let me say how it holds together. The flow is not driven by a model deciding what to do next, which is how these things usually wander off. It is driven by a typed, checkpointed state machine, a deterministic spine, and the models are kept to small, scoped reasoning tasks that happen only after deterministic enrichment has done the plodding factual work. An alert comes in, gets normalised to a common schema, gets enriched, and only then does an agent reason over it and return an evidence-cited verdict. Every state-changing action, every single one, passes through a gate called the Guardian before it reaches a connector. And everything that happens, entry, exit, each grant, the final execution, is written to an append-only, SHA-256 hash-chained case ledger you can verify and replay afterwards.

Allow me one paragraph of respect for the genuinely hard part, because it is where the real work went. A triage case is rarely a straight line. Halfway through, the machine may need a human to approve a containment step, and a human is a slow, unpredictable, entirely necessary thing to wait for. The kernel handles this by suspending the run, checkpointing the exact typed state to disk, and then, when the approval arrives, resuming by re-entering the precise node it left, with the state restored intact. Nothing is replayed from scratch. Nothing is guessed. Getting suspend-and-resume right, so that a case can pause for a person for an hour or a day and then carry on as though no time had passed, is quietly, unglamorously difficult, and it is finished. You start the whole thing with no ceremony at all:

uv sync                 # install
uv run bk serve         # control plane on http://127.0.0.1:8713
uv run bk triage -      # triage one alert from stdin (JSON)
uv run bk eval          # report the false-negative rate, honestly

That runs offline by default, with no API key and no data leaving your machine. Point it at a cloud provider, or a local Ollama, when you are ready.

Now I owe you the disappointing half, and I want to be plain about it, because the honesty is the point rather than a footnote to it. blue-kakapo is not a detector. It reasons over the telemetry and detections you feed it, and if your detection engineering is poor, no amount of clever triage will save you. Reducing the alert count is a comfort that can quietly hide a real threat, so the tool will never pretend that a shorter queue means a safer network. It can miss real threats, because every triage system can. Its bias is to escalate when it is uncertain, and humans stay in the loop on anything consequential, and even so, a false negative remains possible. Local models trail the frontier ones on hard cases. Guardrails reduce prompt-injection risk, they do not abolish it, and the whole design assumes an injection will eventually land and works to limit what it can touch when it does. The hash-chained ledger detects tampering, though a sufficiently privileged database administrator could recompute the entire chain, which is why serious anchoring is an external, optional thing.

I ship the measuring tool rather than a flattering number. Run `bk eval` and it reports precision, recall, calibration, cost per case, and the false-negative rate, the metric that matters most and is most often hidden. On the bundled dataset those figures are labelled an illustrative floor, never a real-world claim, because the sample is small and synthetic and deliberately includes stealthy misses so the miss rate is honest and non-zero. For numbers that mean something about your environment, you run it on your own labelled data. That is the only honest way to quote a figure.

The security posture follows the same temper, because the tool treats its own agents as part of the attack surface. Every agent satisfies a Rule of Two: no single agent may at once process untrusted input, hold sensitive access, and change external state, and construction simply fails if one tries to. The Guardian returns one of five dispositions for any action, allow, deny, modify, ask, or defer, and it defaults to ask. It never fails open. High-impact verbs, isolating a host, disabling a user, killing a process, demand two distinct human approvers on any asset, and the agent that proposes an action is forbidden from approving it, so nothing is ever auto-isolated, crown jewel or not. Personal data is encrypted per record with AES-256-GCM, and destroying the key crypto-shreds it for erasure, with surviving hashes computed over ciphertext so they cannot be brute-forced afterwards. The controls map to the OWASP LLM, Agentic and MCP top-ten lists and to an AISVS self-assessment, and every claim in that assessment is labelled with care: a clean check means no violation was detected, not that a control was proven present. There are 154 tests behind it, including a dedicated adversarial suite. It is Apache-2.0, all of it.

What is it really for, when you strip away the architecture? A coworker you are allowed to check. The deeper comfort here is not the hours the machine gives back, pleasant as those are. It is that when it clears an alert, you can open the ledger and see exactly why, follow the cited evidence across the agents that gathered it, and replay the reasoning that led to the verdict. Trust, in this trade, is not a feeling you are asked to summon. It is a thing you should be able to audit at two in the morning when it counts.

That is the defining virtue of the whole peculiar bird. It does the tedious first reading of an impossible queue, and then, unlike almost everything else sold to you for the job, it keeps the receipts and owns up to what it got wrong.
