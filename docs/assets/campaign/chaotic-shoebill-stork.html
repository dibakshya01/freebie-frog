Think of the last security control you shipped and then stopped thinking about. A service-mesh policy that insists two services prove who they are before they exchange a single byte. A gateway that checks the token on every request and throttles the ones that arrive too fast. A tokenizer that quietly swaps real card numbers for harmless stand-ins before anything they touch can reach a log. You configured it once. You watched it pass a test or two. Then you did the only sensible thing a busy person can do with a thing that works. You trusted it, and you moved on to the next fire.

Here is the uncomfortable part, and I promise to be gentle about it. You have no idea whether any of that still works today.

Not because you were careless. Because controls decay in the dark, and nothing tells you when. A mesh policy reverts to permissive during a migration and nobody notices, because everything keeps working. An allowlist gets widened at midnight for a spot of debugging and never gets narrowed again. A detection rule goes on watching a metric label that a dependency renamed three releases ago, so it fires on precisely nothing. An authorization service hits its timeout and falls back, and the fallback, chosen for availability by someone who meant well, happens to be permissive. That last family has a name in this trade. Fail-open under stress. Highest severity, lowest observability, and almost never tested, because every fallback path on earth was written and drilled for staying up, and hardly any was drilled for staying shut.

So how do most teams actually discover that a control stopped working? One of two ways, both of them expensive. At three in the morning, during the incident the control was supposed to prevent. Or once a year, during an audit that inspects your configuration, which is to say your good intentions, and never once watches your system behave under fire. Both arrive too late. Both cost a fortune. Neither gives you a number you can act on.

So I built a tool that goes and finds out on purpose, on a quiet Tuesday, on your terms.

It is called chaotic-shoebill-stork, which everyone shortens to `shoebill` within about a minute, and it is a Security Chaos Engineering platform. The idea behind the discipline is older than the name and borrowed, honestly, from Netflix. You do not earn confidence that a system is resilient by hoping it is. You earn it by deliberately breaking something in a bounded, controlled way and watching whether the system holds. Reliability engineers have done this for a decade with mature tooling. Doing it to your security controls instead of your servers is the same trick aimed at a better target, and in the open-source world it has been almost entirely untooled. One early attempt was archived in 2019. The grown-up tools that do it are closed, commercial, and priced accordingly. `shoebill` is a clean-room, Apache-2.0 platform built for this one job.

The method is a hypothesis dressed as a question. Your firewall rule, your authorization policy, your detection alert, each one is really a claim: this control will block, or detect, or page on, this bad thing. `shoebill` treats the claim as testable. It safely reproduces the bad thing, watches four separate questions at once, and writes down the answers. Did the control prevent it? Failing that, did it detect it? Did it alert? Did it page a human, and did every stage happen inside the service level objective you promised? Then it rolls the verdicts into a per-service efficacy scorecard, so the honest state of your defences stops being a feeling and becomes a figure you can put on a slide.

The whole thing runs on your laptop, offline, deterministically, start to finish.

shoebill demo            # run the full loop in-process; writes signed evidence + a scorecard
shoebill evidence verify # check the signatures, the hash chain, and the signed head

No Kubernetes, no network, no external anything. The binary is a single static Go file of about nine megabytes with the dashboard embedded inside it.

Now allow me a paragraph of genuine respect for the hard part, because a tool like this lives or dies somewhere most people never look. Injecting the fault is the easy portion. The difficult, unglamorous, genuinely clever work is making the verdict honest. Consider the trap waiting for you. Your run detonates a condition, and at that very moment, by pure coincidence, some unrelated alert fires across the estate. A naive tool reads the noise, sees an alert, and cheerfully records a PASS for a control that did nothing of the kind. So `shoebill` weaves a single run UUID into every artifact it touches, and every assertion is required to correlate on it. A run that merely happens alongside unrelated noise can never score a false success. And when a required signal source goes dark and the platform simply cannot see what happened, the verdict is `INCONCLUSIVE`, and `INCONCLUSIVE` never, under any aggregation, quietly becomes `PASS`. If we could not observe, we learned nothing, and the tool says so. A control that was expected but was never actually tested scores a flat 0.0, not a comforting "unknown". A verification engine that reports success when it could not see is worse than no engine at all, and this one refuses to be that.

Here is where I disappoint you a little, on purpose, because the honesty is the whole point of the exercise.

`shoebill` takes a snapshot. It tells you a control held at the moment of the run, and then it knows nothing at all until the next one, which is exactly why the scorecard decays with age. Green means recently proven, and it says so. It does not prove correctness for all inputs, because it is an experiment and not a theorem. Its coverage is only ever as good as your catalogue and the signal sources you have wired up. The in-cluster detonation, the part that kills containers and rewrites network policy on a live Kubernetes cluster, is a separate operator module that is implemented and unit-tested against fake clients, and has not yet been run end-to-end against real infrastructure. I would rather you heard that from me than discovered it yourself.

The safety posture is structural, which is to say it does not depend on anyone keeping a promise. There is exactly one door to injection, the Safety Governor, and nothing detonates without its explicit yes. It runs ten gates in order and evaluates every one of them, so you see the whole refusal envelope at once instead of fixing failures one at a time. Each detonator independently refuses to act without a capability the Governor has minted: Ed25519-signed, scoped to a single target and action, and time-boxed to the run. The platform cannot widen its own scope. Data-protection experiments use uniquely fingerprinted canary tokens and never real PII, ever. A dead-man's switch forces the fault undone even if the ordinary revert fails. And the evidence an auditor reads is signed and self-describing, carried on a hash chain with a separately-signed head, so it is tamper-evident, reorder-evident, and truncation-evident to anyone holding the public key. They can trust the evidence without having to trust us. There is one honest caveat, written down plainly: it is not protection against a compromised signing key, and strong non-repudiation wants an external witness that v1 does not ship.

What is it all for, underneath? A number you can trust about the one question that actually matters at three in the morning, asked at a time of your choosing, when the stakes are a dashboard and not an outage. The quiet dignity of walking into the audit with signed proof of runtime behaviour instead of a folder of good intentions. The small, real relief of watching `FAIL_OPEN` light up on a Tuesday, when it is a finding, rather than on a Saturday, when it is a breach.

It detonates adversarial conditions for a living, and the most remarkable thing about it is that it refuses to lie to you about the result. That turns out to be the whole of its value.
