You asked the AI for a feature, and the AI delivered. The thing works. It sits there on the screen doing precisely what you wanted, and for a moment you feel the warm flush of a job done. Then a small and faintly accusatory thought arrives, the way such thoughts do. You could not, if pressed, explain how it works. You could not fix it at some unsociable hour when it breaks, and it will break. You could not have written it yourself, and a quiet voice wonders whether you ever will now.

That voice is worth listening to. It is the sound of a skill you were meant to be building, quietly atrophying while a machine did the reps for you.

Here is the trap, laid out plainly. You can learn, which usually means tutorials that walk you through a toy app you will never touch again. Or you can build, which increasingly means an agent ships you a wall of working code you do not understand. Pick learning and you stay a beginner with good intentions. Pick building and you become the owner of software you cannot read, a landlord who has lost the keys to his own house. Both roads are well travelled. Both leave you, in their different ways, dependent.

So I built a third road, and named it after a teacher.

dronAIcharya turns Claude Code, or Cursor, into a patient master-teacher that builds real, production-grade software with you and makes sure you actually understand what the two of you built. The name nods to Dronacharya, the legendary guru of the Mahabharata, because the whole idea rests on one old and unfashionable belief: that the point of the exercise is the student.

It works like a journey you both walk. You pick a teacher from four personas, Vanilla, Serious and Strict, Warm and Witty, or Dry and Deadpan, which change the voice and nothing else. You pick one of four tracks: Vibecoding for the gentlest start, Learn Python, CS from Scratch, or Cybersecurity and AI Security. You pick a cadence, how often the guru stops to teach and check, from Thorough down to Fast. Then you bring an idea, and the rhythm begins: teach the concept, decide the real tradeoffs together, build the next layer, show you what just happened under the hood, check that it stuck, move on. At any moment you can say ELI5, or go deeper, or skip, or pause, and the guru obeys on the instant. Your progress lives in a small `.dronacharya/` folder, so it can always pick up where you left off.

Now let me pay respect to the part that was genuinely hard, because it is the part that decides whether a tool like this lives or dies.

An AI tutor wants to drift. Not out of malice, but out of helpfulness. Twenty minutes into a long session, the model quietly forgets it was meant to teach and simply starts doing, because doing is faster and the context that held the teaching contract has scrolled off into the dark. Worse, Claude Code compacts its own memory as a session grows, and compaction is precisely the moment a tutor's resolve evaporates. Keeping a guru teaching across hours and across compaction, without turning it into a nagging cage you want to throttle, is the real engineering.

dronAIcharya solves it with hooks that re-anchor the teaching contract whenever the ground shifts beneath it.

"SessionStart": {
  "matcher": "startup|resume|compact",
  "command": "scripts/session-start.sh"
}

That one word, `compact`, is the quiet hero. When Claude Code squeezes its memory, the hook fires and feeds the full contract back in, so the guru wakes from the compaction still knowing who it is. A second hook drops a short crumb on every prompt. A third watches for accidental drift at the end of a turn, and it is deliberately timid: it nudges at most once, stays silent on short replies, says nothing if the turn already asked you a question, and never once argues with a pause or a skip or a "just fix this for me." The guardrail is a hand on the shoulder. You remain in charge of the room.

I should be equally plain about what it will not do.

It reduces teaching drift. It does not abolish it, and it will always, every time, obey your "pause" or "skip" or "do it for me this once." The mastery checks are formative self-tests to tell you where you stand, not certificates to frame. The CS track is honest undergraduate foundations, bits and memory and Big-O and how the web actually works, and it stops well short of a degree. And it never enters your credentials, never pays, never buys a domain, never publishes or deploys on your behalf. It preps everything up to the final click, and the click stays yours.

That last restraint is the security posture at work, and the security posture is the thing I am proudest of. dronAIcharya makes no network calls beyond the fetches a lesson openly performs. Your API key stays on your machine. It is never pasted into the chat, never echoed, never written to any file the tool commits, and `.dronacharya/`, `.env`, `.key` and `.pem` are all gitignored. Everything the tool reads from the web or a tool result is treated as data and quarantined, so a poisoned README cannot tell your guru what to do. The only values the hooks ever inject back into the model, your persona and track and cadence, pass through a whitelist that accepts a value only if it exactly matches a short known constant, and otherwise substitutes a safe default. Share a project whose `.dronacharya/progress.json` someone has tampered with, and it still cannot smuggle a single instruction-shaped word into the context. There is a test that proves it. This matters doubly, because lesson one of the security track is prompt injection, and a teacher who preaches the data-and-instruction boundary while leaking all over it would be a fraud.

What is it all really for? Growth, which is a slower reward than a finished feature and a far more compounding one. The aim is that you walk away from each session with a working thing and the understanding of why it works, so the next thing is a little more yours and a little less the machine's. It is the difference between a person who can prompt an agent and a person who can read what the agent wrote and know when to trust it. One of those people is renting their competence. The other owns it.

It is MIT licensed, every skill a plain file you can open and read before you trust it. It teaches while it builds, it tells you the truth about its own limits, and it hands you back the keys to your own house.

The guru's defining virtue is that it refuses to let you go passive. In an age busy automating people out of their own skills, that refusal is the whole point.
