Picture the moment, because the odds are good you have lived some version of it. You are deep in a refactor that is finally going well. The agent and you have found a rhythm, it proposes, you nudge, it writes, the tests go green. Then a small grey message slides into the terminal, polite as a traffic warden, and informs you that you have reached your usage limit for the day.

The rhythm dies on the spot.

Now do the sum, because the sum is where it stings. On that same desk, very probably, sits capacity going quietly to waste. Two or three model providers whose free tiers you signed up for months ago and never once touched. Perhaps a capable machine beside you running a coding model that costs nothing but the electricity. A laptop whose fans are, at that exact moment, entirely idle. All of it within reach in principle, and not a scrap of it reachable in practice, because the one agent you actually wanted to use knows how to talk to precisely one company's front door and no other.

That is a very stupid problem to have. It is also, when you hold it up to the light and turn it over, a problem about language far more than a problem about money.

Watch the shape of it. A coding agent such as Claude Code speaks a particular dialect when it talks to a model. It phrases everything in the Anthropic Messages format, a specific grammar with specific expectations. The free and local models I wanted to reach speak a different dialect, usually the OpenAI-compatible one, sometimes Google's Gemini variant. Same conversation, same intent, incompatible grammar. Two parties who would happily talk, kept apart for want of an interpreter.

So I built the interpreter. It is called freebie-frog, it is one small Go binary, and it sits quietly in the middle of that conversation and translates both ways. The agent goes on speaking the only language it knows. The model answers in the only language it knows. Neither one ever notices a translator was in the room. You point your agent at the frog with a single environment variable, and you carry on.

go install github.com/dibakshya01/freebie-frog/cmd/freebie@latest
export GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_...
freebie run claude

Three lines, and the agent that was just shown the door is running happily on Groq's free tier. Swap the key and it runs on Gemini, or DeepSeek, or that idle model on the desk through Ollama. The refactor resumes. The rhythm comes back.

I could stop the story there, and a good many tools like this one do. They forward the request, they forward the reply, they take a bow. But that is the easy ninety percent, and the easy ninety percent is not where these things live or die. They die in the streaming.

Allow me one paragraph of respect for the hard part, because it is genuinely hard and it is where the real work went.

When a model answers, it does not hand you a finished paragraph. It dribbles the reply out token by token, a stream of small events, so your screen can fill in real time. That much everyone knows. What is less appreciated is that the two dialects disagree, in fussy and maddening detail, about the choreography of that stream. They disagree on the order the events arrive in. They disagree on how each fragment is shaped. And they disagree most of all about the nastiest case of the lot, which is when the model decides to call a tool and the arguments to that tool arrive in pieces, a few characters of JSON at a time, to be reassembled on the far end.

Get that reassembly wrong by a single fragment and the failure is not loud. It is quiet, and it is awful. The agent either freezes, waiting forever for an event in a shape that will never come, or it acts with total confidence on a tool call that is subtly malformed and does the wrong thing beautifully. Either way the thread is lost, and worse, you may not notice it is lost.

freebie-frog's real work is getting that choreography exactly right. It takes whatever the provider sends, in whatever order and shape the provider favours, and reconstructs from it the precise event sequence the agent expects, tool-call fragments and all. The stream your agent reads is the stream it would have read if it had been talking to its home provider the whole time. That is the entire game. It is quietly, unglamorously difficult, and it is finished.

So far this has been a cheerful story. Here is where I disappoint you a little, on purpose, because the disappointment is the single most important thing in this whole piece.

freebie-frog will not make models free. It will not make them unlimited. And I want to be very plain about that, because a whole genre of tool in this corner of the internet is anything but plain about it.

You have seen the promises. "Unlimited AI." "One billion free tokens, no card required." They are seductive, and they are, in the main, a conjuring trick. You deliver a billion free tokens by quietly creating and cycling through a crowd of throwaway accounts, each one burning its free allowance before being discarded for the next in the queue. There is no clever efficiency hiding behind the curtain. There is only a violation of the provider's terms of service, wearing a product's clothes and hoping nobody checks the pockets.

freebie-frog does none of that, by design. It uses your keys, your accounts, one of each. The free tiers it reaches are the providers' own, with the providers' own limits, which the providers can and do change whenever they please. Add them all up honestly and you land somewhere in the range of tens to hundreds of millions of tokens a month, throttled by the minute and by the day. That is a very great deal of coding for most of us. It is not infinity, and I will not tell you it is, because a number you can trust is worth a good deal more than a number that merely sounds nice.

That, when you boil it down, is the whole argument. An honest tool is a more useful tool. You can plan around a real limit. You cannot plan around a fib, and you certainly cannot plan around an account-rotation scheme that will stop working the morning a provider decides to care.

A word on the security, while we are being plain, because I built this the way I would want a colleague to have built it before handing it to me. Outbound calls are checked against server-side request forgery, so a poisoned config file cannot quietly turn the proxy into a weapon aimed at your own internal network. Secrets live in your operating system's keychain, or an encrypted file, and never once in a log. The thing binds to localhost and stays there until you knowingly open it up. There is a small dashboard for watching your requests and the health of each provider, and it is read-only, and it guards against DNS rebinding, because a dashboard that can rewrite your keys from a web page is a dashboard that is waiting to be sorry.

What is it all for, in the end? Choice, mostly, which is a quieter virtue than "unlimited" but a far more durable one. The freedom to run the day's dull, routine work on a free tier or a local model, and keep the expensive provider for the jobs that genuinely earn it. The small, real comfort that when one free service is busy, the frog simply tries the next one on your list and you keep typing. And the quieter comfort still of pointing the whole arrangement at a model on your own machine, where your code never leaves the building at all.

It is Apache-2.0. It is clean-room, which is precisely why the permissive licence is valid and not merely a hope. It is a single binary that does one difficult thing well and tells you the truth about the things it cannot do.

The frog is honest about its own limits. That turns out to be the most useful thing about it.
